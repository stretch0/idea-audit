# Frameworks, and where each one misleads

Use these to articulate a position you have evidenced. None of them will find
one for you.

## Porter's Five Forces

Rivalry, threat of entry, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power.

**Good for** describing why an industry is or isn't structurally profitable.

**Where it misleads:**

- **It is industry-average.** It tells you about a sector, not about whether
  one position in it works for one operator.
- **It is static.** It cannot tell you how fast the forces move, and in a
  low-barrier market they move fast by definition. A "niche and underserved"
  reading can be wrong within a year.
- **It says nothing about demand.** You can complete a full analysis without
  ever asking whether anyone wants the thing.
- **It assumes your constraint is capital allocation.** A solo operator's
  constraints are distribution and time, which it does not model.
- **It is only as good as your list of players.** Five Forces takes the
  competitive set as given. Run it against an incomplete map and it returns a
  confident answer about a market that does not exist — so run
  `/idea-audit:competitor-scan` first, not after.

**If you use it:** add supplier power explicitly for any reselling business,
and date the analysis. Re-run it before committing anything large.

## SWOT

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.

**Good for** communicating a position to people who already know the domain.

**Where it misleads:** it invites symmetry — four boxes implying four equal
quadrants — when in practice one threat usually dominates everything else. It
produces lists rather than decisions, and it has no mechanism for ranking or
falsifying anything in them.

**If you use it:** rank every entry, and force a single sentence on which one
matters most and why.

## Rumelt's kernel — *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*

1. **Diagnosis** — what is actually going on
2. **Guiding policy** — the approach chosen in response
3. **Coherent action** — the steps, which must not contradict each other

The best available test of whether something is a strategy or a slogan. "Niche
and trust-led" is a slogan until the diagnosis and the actions are specified.

## 7 Powers — Helmer

Scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs,
branding, cornered resource, process power.

Sharper than Porter's for the question that actually matters to a small
operator — *is my position defensible?* — because each power is a specific
mechanism you either have or do not.

Worth adding an eighth, informally: **incentive misalignment**. A feature an
incumbent will not build because it costs them revenue is defensible in a way
novelty is not.

## Jobs to be Done

What is the customer hiring this to do? Expressed as their job, not your
feature.

The best JTBD statements are found, not written — they appear verbatim in
customer language. A job stated in your own words is usually a feature in
disguise.

## Wardley-style evolution

Ask where each component sits on the path from genesis to commodity, and which
way it is moving.

Answers the question Porter's misses: not "is this attractive now?" but "will
this still be a business in three years?" A component drifting toward utility
is a poor place to hold a margin.
