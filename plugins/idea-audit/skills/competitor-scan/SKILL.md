# Competitor scan

Positioning analysis is only as good as the list of players it runs against. A
confident Five Forces read on an incomplete set produces a confident wrong
answer — and the most expensive version is proposing as your differentiator
something a specialist already sells.

**Do this before positioning, not after.** And treat "nobody does this" as a
claim to be disproved, not an assumption to build on.

## Search as the customer, not as the founder

Founders search in supplier language — "API", "wholesale", "platform". The
customer searches in problem language. Only the second surfaces the companies
the customer actually meets.

Write down the literal sentence a customer would type, then search that.

## Nine places the full list hides

1. **Comparison and "best X for Y" articles.** Maintained by people paid to be
   complete. One page often names more of the market than a week of your own
   searching.
2. **Category directories.** Most verticals have one, sometimes with
   exhaustive listings.
3. **The communities.** Ask what people actually use. Real users name brands no
   analyst list contains — and their answers are current.
4. **The partner and customer pages of whatever you depend on.** Whoever
   supplies your API, platform or stock supplies your competitors too, and
   their case studies, integration directories and partner announcements
   enumerate them for free.
5. **App stores.** Search the category, then read the one- and two-star reviews
   of the top ten.
6. **Reviews and complaints about incumbents.** These reveal both the unmet
   need and the also-rans people tried first.
7. **Who is paying for ads** on the customer's query. Someone spending money
   believes the market is real.
8. **Adjacent and free substitutes.** Not just companies: the thing they
   already own, the thing bundled with something else, doing it manually, doing
   nothing.
9. **General-purpose AI.** For anything whose value is matching, comparison,
   research or explanation, put the customer's question to a chatbot and judge
   the answer as a customer would. It is now a competitor and it appears on no
   market map.

## Sort what you find

| Type | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Direct | Same job, same customer, same shape |
| Specialist | Same niche, deeper — the dangerous one |
| Generalist moving in | Big, funded, recently entered |
| Substitute | Solves the job differently, often already owned |
| Free / DIY | Community knowledge, manual effort, free alternatives |
| Aggregator | Comparison sites that own the customer's attention |

Specialists and generalists-moving-in are the ones most often missed, and the
most damaging to miss.

## Build the matrix

One row per player, one column per capability that matters. Fill it from their
own pages, not from summaries.

Then take every claimed differentiator and **try to disprove it**. Search
specifically for someone doing that exact thing. If a claim survives a
determined attempt to kill it, it is worth something. If you have not tried,
you do not have a differentiator — you have a hypothesis.

## Ask why the gap exists

A genuine gap has a reason. Find it, then judge whether it persists.

- **Incentive misalignment** — it costs an incumbent revenue to build. Durable,
  because noticing it does not change their incentive.
- **Too small to matter** — real, until you make it look bigger.
- **Technically hard** — check whether that is still true.
- **Regulatory** — durable while it lasts.
- **Nobody has got to it yet** — the weakest. Assume a year.

## Date everything, and re-run it

Competitive facts perish. A specialist can enter a niche and a well-funded
generalist can launch a product line inside a year, which is faster than most
analyses are refreshed.

Record when you looked. Re-run before any large commitment, and specifically
re-check every claim of the form "nobody does this".

## Output

- The full list, sorted by type, each with a source and a date
- The capability matrix
- For every claimed differentiator: who else does it, or the record of a
  genuine attempt to find someone
- The gap's cause, and how long it survives
- What you would have to believe for this market to still have room

## The failure this prevents

The pattern is always the same shape. A plan names a capability as its
differentiator — the thing no competitor offers — and a specialist in an
adjacent segment has been shipping exactly that for years. The analysis was
careful, the framework was applied properly, and the map was missing one
company, so the proposed differentiator was somebody else's core product.

It is rarely the obvious competitor that gets missed. It is the specialist one
segment over, or the generalist who launched a product line last quarter, or
the free thing the customer already has.
