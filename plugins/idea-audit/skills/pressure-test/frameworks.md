# Applying the frameworks

Operational notes, loaded when a skill needs to apply one of these. Not an
explainer — assume the framework is already understood. This covers how to run
it in an idea audit, what the output must contain, and the limitation to state
alongside it so nobody mistakes the output for a finding.

These are analysis frameworks, used last, to articulate a position already
evidenced. The demand phase's own method and its limits live in
`/idea-audit:customer-research`.

Rule for all of them: **name specific actors and cite evidence.** A force, a
power or a quadrant filled in abstractly is decoration.


---

## Porter's Five Forces

**Use for** describing whether a sector is structurally profitable. Only after
the market map is complete.

**Run it:** for each force name the actual companies or actors and the evidence
— prices, terms, headcount, funding. Add a direction of travel: is this force
strengthening or weakening, and how fast?

**Output:** five short paragraphs, each naming who and why, each with a
direction. Plus one sentence on which force dominates.

**Always state alongside:**
- It is industry-average, not position-specific. A sector can be unattractive
  and one position in it fine, or the reverse.
- It is static. In a low-barrier market the forces move faster than the
  analysis is refreshed.
- It is silent on demand. You can complete it without establishing that anyone
  wants the thing.
- It assumes the constraint is capital allocation. For a small operator it is
  usually distribution and time.
- It is only as good as the list of players, so run `/idea-audit:competitor-scan`
  first.

**Failure mode:** reporting a sector as "attractive" as though that were a
decision. It is a description of an industry average at one moment, and says
nothing about whether any position in it is available to this founder.

For any business reselling or intermediating, add **supplier power** explicitly
— it is where consolidation and margin compression show up first.

## Porter's generic strategies (the matrix)

Two axes — advantage (cost or differentiation) against scope (broad or narrow)
— giving cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus and differentiation
focus. Distinct from Five Forces: the forces describe whether a sector is
profitable, the matrix describes which position a player occupies in it.

**Use for** stating a chosen position compactly, after the market map is
complete and demand is evidenced. Not for finding the gap.

**Run it:** place every player from `/idea-audit:competitor-scan` in a cell,
each with the evidence that put it there. Then place the proposed position, and
name who else is in that cell.

**Output:** the populated matrix, the proposed cell, and the occupants of that
cell. If the proposed cell is empty, an explicit answer to why — see below.

**Always state alongside. The first two are not optional — give them even
when the answer is being kept short:**

- **An empty cell is not a gap.** The matrix plots chosen positions, not
  demand. A cell is empty because nobody has reached it *or* because it is not
  viable, and the matrix cannot tell those apart. Establishing which is the job
  of the demand phase and the competitor scan, and the matrix must not be used
  to skip either.
- **For a small operator the two broad cells do not exist.** Cost leadership
  against a funded incumbent is not a strategy, and broad differentiation needs
  a marketing budget to match. The real choice is between the two focus cells,
  which reduces the whole matrix to "pick a niche" — say that plainly rather
  than presenting four live options to someone who has two.
- The axes are chosen by whoever draws it. Add enough dimensions of
  "differentiated" and every player occupies a unique cell, so a matrix showing
  the proposal alone in a quadrant is usually a statement about the axes.
- "Stuck in the middle" is Porter's own claim and is contested; hybrid
  positions demonstrably exist.
- It is static. It says nothing about whether a cell's occupant will stay
  there, or whether a generalist is about to enter it.

**Failure mode:** choosing focused differentiation because the cell looked
empty, when the cell was empty because the niche is too small to support a
business, or because a specialist who never made the map is already in it. This
is the single most common way a careful analysis produces a confident wrong
answer, and it is why the competitor scan runs first.

## 7 Powers

**Use for** the question "is this defensible?" — sharper than Five Forces
because each power is a mechanism you either have or do not.

**Run it:** test all seven against this specific business — scale economies,
network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered
resource, process power. Expect most to be absent. That is the useful result.

Add an eighth informally: **incentive misalignment** — a feature an incumbent
is structurally unwilling to build because it costs them revenue. Durable,
because noticing it does not change their incentive.

**Output:** which powers exist today with evidence, which could be built and
how long that takes, which will never apply.

**Failure mode:** claiming a power you aspire to. "We will have switching
costs" is not a power; it is a plan.

## Rumelt's kernel

**Use for** testing whether a stated strategy is a strategy or a slogan.

**Run it:** extract three things — the **diagnosis** (what is actually going
on), the **guiding policy** (the approach chosen in response), the **coherent
actions** (steps that do not contradict each other). If any is missing, or if
the guiding policy is a restated goal, it is a slogan.

**Output:** the three, or a plain statement of which is missing and what would
fill it.

**Failure mode:** accepting aspiration as policy. "Be the trusted option" names
a desired outcome, not an approach.

## Jobs to be Done

**Use for** stating what the customer is hiring this to do.

**Run it:** find the job in customer language from research. Do not write one.
A job you composed is a feature in disguise.

**Output:** one sentence in their words, with the source it came from. The
best ones are unglamorous and specific.

**Failure mode:** a feature restated. "They want itinerary matching" is a
feature; "they want to know they can get back to the meeting point in time" is
a job.

## SWOT

**Use for** communicating a position to someone who already knows the domain.
Analytically the weakest tool here.

**Run it:** rank every entry. Four boxes imply four balanced quadrants; in
practice one threat usually dominates everything else.

**Output:** ranked lists, plus one sentence naming the single entry that
matters most and why.

**Failure mode:** symmetry read as balance, and no mechanism for ranking or
falsifying anything in it.

---

## Evolution mapping

**Use for** "will this still be a business in three years?" — the question
Five Forces cannot answer.

**Run it:** place each component your idea depends on somewhere between genesis
and commodity, and note which way it is moving. Self-serve suppliers,
converging prices and interchangeable vendors all mean commoditising.

**Output:** which components are commoditising, on what evidence, and what
that does to margin over the horizon you care about.

**Failure mode:** assessing only today. A component drifting toward utility is
a poor place to hold a margin, however comfortable it looks now.
