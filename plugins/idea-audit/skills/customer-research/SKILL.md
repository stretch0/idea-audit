# Customer research

Demand evidence is the cheapest research available and almost always gets done
last. Do it before supply integration, competitor analysis or architecture.

## Listen before you ask

Start where people are already discussing the problem unprompted: forums,
subreddits, review sections, support communities, question sites. This beats
interviews, because nobody was performing for an interviewer.

Extract only three things per thread:

- **What they did** — the actual behaviour, not the stated preference
- **What it cost them** — money, time, or a bad day
- **Their exact words** — these become the copy later

Look for the emotional register. "I've been putting this off", "I feel stupid",
"I was scared of the bill" are stronger signals than any feature request.

### Check the dates

A finding's shelf life varies enormously:

| Finding | Shelf life |
| --- | --- |
| Emotional friction, confusion | Years |
| Prices | Months |
| Which competitor is best | Months |
| A specific gap or missing feature | Weeks — someone may have closed it |

Always re-check the perishable ones. Sort by newest, count how recent the
stream is, and say plainly how old the evidence is. A two-year-old thread is
evidence that the problem *existed*.

### Count the frequency

Volume matters as much as existence. Search the community for the key term,
sort by newest, and measure the rate — posts per week, and how far back page
one reaches. Distinguish *posts* from *threads*: one long thread can generate
many mentions and overstate the population.

## Then ask, without poisoning the answer

The instinct is to describe the idea and ask if they like it. That produces
compliments, which are worthless.

| Don't ask | Ask instead |
| --- | --- |
| "Would you use a tool that…" | "What did you do last time?" |
| "Is X hard?" | "Walk me through how you decided." |
| "Would you pay £N?" | "What did it end up costing you?" |
| "Is this a good idea?" | "What was annoying about it?" |

Everything past tense, everything specific, nothing about your product. The
follow-ups matter more than the openers: *"What did you try before that?"*,
*"Who told you to do it that way?"*, *"How long did that take?"*

Start with people who are easy to reach. The first three conversations will be
badly run, and you want to be bad at them cheaply.

## Score commitment, not enthusiasm

The currency is something that costs them:

- They hand over contact details unprompted
- They introduce you to someone else with the problem
- They spend real time on it
- **They have already spent money trying to solve it** — the strongest signal

"Great idea", "I'd definitely use that" and "let me know when it launches" with
no contact details are all noise.

## Decide the stop condition before you start

Write down, in advance, what result means stop. Without it you will
rationalise whatever you find.

A workable default: *if nobody asks to buy within N conversations, stop*. One
person asking "where do I get this" outweighs a hundred anonymous approvals.

Ten to fifteen conversations is usually where patterns appear.

## Report honestly

Separate what was observed from what was inferred. Quote verbatim. Say which
claims are load-bearing and unevidenced. Include the findings that hurt — they
are the ones that change decisions.
