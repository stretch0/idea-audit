# Pressure-test a business idea

Run the phases in order. Stop at the first one that fails — the value is in
killing things cheaply, not in completing the analysis.

The most common failure is doing this backwards: weeks of supply-side and
competitor research, frameworks producing a confident answer, and no contact
with a customer until after the product exists.

## Phase 0 — Kill screen

Run the `kill-screen` skill first. Five questions, under an hour, and most
ideas end here. Do not proceed to anything below until it passes.

## Phase 1 — Demand

Before suppliers, before competitors, before architecture.

Run the `customer-research` skill. You are answering one question: **does
anyone have this problem, in their own words, recently?**

If demand cannot be evidenced, nothing downstream matters. An idea that reaches
Phase 4 without a single customer quote is an idea nobody has checked.

## Phase 2 — Substitutes

Not "who are the competitors" but **"what does this person do today?"** —
including doing nothing, using something free, or using a thing they already
pay for.

Price the substitute as the customer experiences it. Then find the scenario
where you lose, and say so plainly. Most ideas have one; an idea whose
advocates cannot name theirs has not been examined.

## Phase 3 — Defensibility

"Nobody does this" is not a moat. Ask why not, and whether that reason persists.

The strongest answer is **incentive misalignment**: a feature the incumbent is
structurally unwilling to build because it costs them revenue. Telling a
customer "we cannot help you" reduces conversion, so companies paid on
conversion do not build it. That survives being noticed, which novelty does not.

Weaker but real: switching costs, accumulating proprietary data, network
effects, a licence you hold, a brand in a trust-critical category, or a market
too small to be worth an incumbent's attention.

Ask also: **is this component commoditising?** Anything drifting toward
utility — where suppliers are becoming self-serve and interchangeable — is a
poor place to build a durable margin, however attractive it looks today.

## Phase 4 — Channel

Usually the binding constraint, and usually assumed rather than solved.

- Is the search space already held by better-resourced operators?
- Are the customers **enumerable**? A list you can work through beats any
  amount of content strategy.
- Does a free, targeted channel exist — a community where the problem is
  already discussed?
- Paid channels: what does one customer cost, and can the unit economics carry
  it? Commission-only affiliate deals compete against whatever incumbents
  already pay, and an unproven brand offers worse expected earnings at the same
  rate — so they select for the partners with the least to lose.

Note that being cited in AI answers is largely a third-party-page phenomenon,
so a community presence and AI visibility are often the same work.

## Phase 5 — Frameworks, last

Use these to *articulate* a position you have already evidenced, never to
discover one. See `reference/frameworks.md` for how to run each and where each
misleads.

- **Rumelt's kernel** — diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. The best
  test of whether a strategy is a strategy or a slogan.
- **7 Powers** — the sharper tool for "is this defensible?"
- **Jobs to be Done** — what is the customer hiring this to do?
- **Porter's Five Forces** — industry-average and static. Useful for
  describing a sector, weak for deciding a position. State its limits whenever
  you use it.
- **SWOT** — widely understood, analytically the weakest here. It invites
  symmetry and produces lists rather than decisions. Use it to communicate,
  not to decide.

## Output

Write a short document, not a deck:

1. **The diagnosis** — what is actually going on, in one paragraph
2. **Evidence** — what was observed, quoted, with dates
3. **Assumptions** — every load-bearing claim with no evidence behind it
4. **The scenario where we lose**
5. **Verdict** — proceed, proceed with named risks, or stop
6. **The cheapest next test**, with a stop condition decided in advance

Mark inference as inference. If a conclusion rests on a marketing page rather
than documentation, say so.

## Anti-patterns

- Supply-side research before demand evidence
- Treating a framework's output as a finding
- Trusting undated sources, or any source about a perishable fact
- Assuming the competitor is the substitute
- Skipping "does a chatbot already do this?"
- Asking gating questions last
- Switching ideas to escape a channel problem — the next idea usually has the
  same channel problem plus harder ones
- Continuing on an idea you have stopped believing in, because a plan that
  needs weeks of unpaid effort cannot run on low conviction
